\section{Introduction}\label{sec:literature_review_introduction}

The review of literature and previous studies is a fundamental pillar and a
methodological starting point in building the theoretical framework for
technical projects. It clarifies the concepts, system models, and prior
practices that define the intellectual setting of the proposed work and helps
distinguish between established educational approaches and the direction
adopted by the project itself.

For EduVerse, that grounding must be drawn from several connected areas rather
than from one isolated topic. Digital education platforms and learning
management systems explain organized academic coordination; communication and
live-teaching tools clarify continuity of interaction; AI in education frames
assisted learning; role-based governance explains controlled differentiation
between users; mobile companion learning informs client asymmetry; and
extensible platform architecture explains how related services can remain
integrated without becoming rigid. Together, these strands provide the
theoretical and comparative basis for EduVerse as a governed educational
environment.

\section{Evolution of Digital Education Platforms}
\label{sec:evolution_of_digital_education_platforms}

\subsection{From Traditional Classroom Management to Digital Learning Systems}
\label{subsec:traditional_to_digital_learning_systems}

Educational management has gradually moved from paper-based and face-to-face
coordination toward persistent digital systems capable of organizing materials,
communication, submissions, and academic records at institutional scale.
Learning management systems emerged as a response to this transition, offering
shared digital spaces through which institutions could manage teaching and
learning more consistently \cite{chugh2023edtech,bygstad2022digital}.

This shift changed more than storage practices. It also changed expectations.
Students and teachers increasingly expect educational systems to support
continuous access, asynchronous participation, traceable communication, and
structured academic follow-up rather than isolated one-time exchanges
\cite{chugh2023edtech}. A modern platform is therefore judged not only by
whether it stores resources, but by whether it supports continuity across the
full educational workflow.

\subsection{Limitations of Fragmented Learning Management Tools}
\label{subsec:limitations_of_fragmented_learning_management_tools}

Despite the growth of digital platforms, many institutions still depend on
separate tools for different academic tasks. One system may handle materials
and class announcements, another live meetings, another whiteboard
collaboration, and another programming practice. This arrangement often works
functionally, but it fragments the learning experience and weakens the
institutional continuity of the workflow \cite{bygstad2022digital,oecd2023outlook}.

This fragmentation is visible in common tool combinations. Google Classroom can
serve as a reference example for class coordination and materials, Google Meet
for live meetings and presentations, Excalidraw-like tools for collaborative
whiteboard work, and LeetCode-like environments for coding practice. These
systems are not discussed here as products to be copied. They are used only as
comparison examples showing how related educational activities are often
distributed across separate technical environments.

When communication, content access, synchronous teaching, assessment follow-up,
and practical work are divided in this way, students and teachers repeatedly
switch context. The result is weaker continuity between related tasks, less
consistent governance over permissions and visibility, repeated duplication of
context across systems, and more difficulty maintaining one coherent
institutional view of learning activity and responsibility
\cite{bygstad2022digital,oecd2023outlook}.

\section{Research Gap and Proposed Integrated Learning Ecosystem}
\label{sec:research_gap_integrated_ecosystem}

The research gap addressed by EduVerse is clear: many educational platforms
solve individual problems well, but fewer systems integrate those solutions
into one governed workspace. Existing tools may support communication, live
teaching, whiteboard explanation, learning materials, coding practice, or AI
assistance separately. However, institutions still need a platform that can
coordinate those functions with differentiated roles, organization boundaries,
feature controls, preserved academic continuity, and a clear line of
responsibility for access, visibility, and workflow transitions.

EduVerse is proposed as a response to that gap. Its contribution is not the
mere presence of familiar features, but the integration of those features in a
multi-organization educational environment. Within that environment, learning
management, communication, live collaboration, assessment, AI support, coding
practice, public or open access, and extensions are treated as related parts of
one academic system rather than as disconnected tools.

The same gap also has a governance dimension. Institutions often need some
features to be optional, restricted, or unavailable in specific contexts. For
that reason, EduVerse is designed around organization-level and class-level
feature availability, including the ability to disable AI where it is not
wanted. Integration in EduVerse therefore does not mean forcing the same tool
set onto every learning context. It means keeping related workflows inside one
shared platform while allowing controlled variation within that platform.

The same gap has an architectural dimension as well. When data and workflow
rules are scattered across unrelated tools, future integration becomes harder
because each new client or service must rebuild institutional behavior from the
outside. EduVerse addresses this by centering its ecosystem on a protected
shared API model that can support web, mobile, and later reuse scenarios
without duplicating academic business rules.

This integrated approach also supports client asymmetry without reintroducing
fragmentation. The web platform remains the primary location of deep
administrative and instructional workflows, while the mobile client is treated
as a companion for selected high-frequency tasks. The goal is one coherent
ecosystem, not identical behavior on every device.

\section{Artificial Intelligence in Education}
\label{sec:artificial_intelligence_in_education}

Artificial intelligence has become increasingly relevant to education because it
can support explanation, summarization, drafting, and personalized academic
assistance when used in pedagogically responsible ways
\cite{bond2024ai,labadze2023chatbots}. The key question is not whether AI
can generate text, but whether it can support learning without weakening
teacher judgment, academic integrity, or institutional control.

EduVerse approaches this issue by treating the AI Agent as a governed academic
service rather than as an unrestricted external chatbot. Its role is to support
students and teachers within the class context, not to replace class materials,
assessment rules, or instructional authority.

\subsection{Context-Aware Learning Assistance}
\label{subsec:context_aware_learning_assistance}

AI support is most educationally useful when it is context aware. Responses
grounded in the learner's current class, materials, or task are more relevant
than generic answers disconnected from the academic environment
\cite{alsafari2024teaching,labadze2023chatbots}. Context awareness therefore improves not only convenience,
but also pedagogical alignment.

In EduVerse, this principle supports the idea of an embedded AI Agent. Rather
than directing students to a separate external service, the platform places AI
help inside the educational workspace so that assistance can remain closer to
the active learning context.

\subsection{Material Summarization and Learning Support}
\label{subsec:material_summarization_and_learning_support}

Long educational documents can create cognitive overload, especially when
students need to recover the structure of a topic quickly. AI-supported
summaries can help by foregrounding key ideas and reducing the effort required
to re-enter the material \cite{xie2025summarization}. This is particularly
useful when materials are numerous or dense.

At the same time, summarization should remain supportive rather than
substitutive. In EduVerse, summarized output is understood as a learning aid
that helps students navigate source material more effectively, not as a
replacement for the original resource.

\subsection{Assessment and Feedback Support}
\label{subsec:assessment_and_feedback_support}

The literature shows that AI can contribute to formative support, drafting, and
feedback preparation when used within clear educational boundaries
\cite{labadze2023chatbots}. However, it also warns that unrestricted use may weaken
authentic assessment and blur the distinction between assistance and completion
\cite{unesco2023genai,labadze2023chatbots}.

EduVerse adopts the more cautious interpretation. AI can support study
guidance, material understanding, and selected drafting tasks, but it should
not override teacher authority or provide direct final answers in active
assessment contexts. This position aligns with the broader thesis argument that
AI should be integrated into educational platforms as a governed feature rather
than treated as an unlimited external shortcut.

\section{Theoretical and Technical Foundations of EduVerse}
\label{sec:theoretical_technical_foundations_eduverse}

The preceding sections establish why EduVerse is framed as an integrated
educational ecosystem. The following subsections identify the main theoretical
and technical foundations that support that position.

\subsection{Web-Based Educational Platforms}
\label{subsec:web_based_educational_platforms}

Web platforms remain central to comprehensive educational systems because they
support rich administrative interfaces, detailed authoring workflows, and
persistent academic records. Their value lies not only in availability through
a browser, but in their ability to coordinate complex institutional logic
within one environment.

For EduVerse, the web client serves as the primary operational surface. It is
the most suitable location for organization management, class administration,
assessment control, and other workflows that require more depth than a thin
mobile interface can comfortably provide.

\subsection{Mobile Companion Learning Applications}
\label{subsec:mobile_companion_learning_applications}

Mobile educational applications are often most effective when they support
high-frequency participation rather than attempt to reproduce every feature of a
full academic platform. Notifications, quick updates, material access, class
chat, and entry into live sessions are examples of tasks that align naturally
with mobile usage \cite{rangel2023mobile}.

EduVerse follows this companion model. The mobile client extends access to
selected workflows while the web platform retains broader administrative and
instructional depth. The same model also benefits from shared protected APIs,
because mobility can then extend the ecosystem without creating a separate
governance logic for academic data and access rules.

\subsection{Real-Time Virtual Classrooms}
\label{subsec:real_time_virtual_classrooms}

Synchronous online teaching remains important because it supports immediacy,
responsiveness, and a stronger sense of instructional presence
\cite{lee2024synchronous}. However, live sessions become less effective when
they are disconnected from the class context that surrounds them.

EduVerse responds by embedding live sessions within the broader class
environment. In this model, synchronous teaching is not a detached event; it is
one part of an ongoing academic workflow that also includes communication,
materials, whiteboard activity, and follow-up.

\subsection{Integrated Coding Environments for Programming Education}
\label{subsec:integrated_coding_environments_programming_education}

Programming education highlights the cost of fragmentation very clearly. When
instruction and coding practice are separated across unrelated systems,
students must repeatedly switch between explanation and execution. An
integrated coding environment helps reduce that gap by keeping practice closer
to the surrounding academic context \cite{noor2023ide}.

EduVerse follows this idea through its browser-based IDE and coding tools. The
importance of this design choice lies not in copying external coding platforms,
but in preserving continuity between explanation, practice, and class context
as part of the wider learning-support environment.

\subsection{Extensible Educational Ecosystems and Governed Openness}
\label{subsec:extensible_ecosystems_governed_openness}

Modular platform thinking is valuable in education because institutions rarely
need every tool in every context. Service-modularity approaches support this kind
of modularity by allowing capabilities to be combined without forcing a rigid
all-or-nothing structure \cite{sorkun2022modularity}. What matters, however, is
that extensibility remains governed.

EduVerse treats extensibility as controlled configuration. Features can be
enabled or disabled at organization or class level, and extensions can be made
available without turning the platform into an unbounded collection of add-ons.

The same logic applies to openness. Public or open-learning access is useful
only when it remains subject to clear institutional rules. A governed platform
can support broader sharing more effectively than a fragmented tool set because
public visibility, internal authority, feature boundaries, and extension
behavior are all managed within one architecture \cite{oecd2023outlook}.

\subsection{Security, Roles, and Data Management}
\label{subsec:security_roles_and_data_management}

Once digital education is treated as a governed ecosystem, role management
becomes a foundational design issue. Role-Based Access Control offers an
established way to separate permissions and responsibilities for different
actors \cite{kabier2023rbac}. In educational settings, such
distinctions are necessary because students, teachers, and administrators do
not interact with academic data from the same position.

EduVerse applies this logic through organization-aware and class-aware access
control, multi-role membership, and selected-role operation. The result is a
platform in which governance remains explicit rather than implicit.

Data management is equally important. Educational platforms must preserve
records, protect sensitive information, and support structured access to
materials and results \cite{liu2023privacy,kabier2023rbac}. EduVerse reflects this need through the separation of
core academic records from larger instructional files and through the
maintenance of academic history under controlled access conditions.

Together, these foundations justify EduVerse as a unified and extensible
educational platform. The next chapter builds on this literature by explaining
the methodology used to analyze, design, implement, and refine the system.
